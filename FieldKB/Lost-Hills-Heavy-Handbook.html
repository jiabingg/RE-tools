Start here

Lost Hills Heavy is a shallow, unconsolidated, 10–19° API steamflood. Almost everything that goes right or wrong here traces back to one of three facts: the sand is very shallow, the oil is very viscous, and the rock has no strength. Hold those three and most of the field's behaviour becomes predictable.

Three facts that explain the field

Shallow. Top of Tulare T1A sits near 200 ft at the crest; East Flank patterns average ~300 ft. Shallow means a low fracture gradient, which means a hard ceiling on injection pressure, which means limited injectivity and a permanent risk of steam reaching surface.

Viscous and cold. Native reservoir temperature is ~82 °F. Cold, high-saturation oil resists steam injection until enough heat is in the rock — modelling shows injectivity stays constrained through the first 0.28–0.37 pore volumes injected. That is why every new pattern is cycle-steamed before continuous injection.

Unconsolidated. Porosity 34–48%, permeability up to several darcies, and cores that are "poorly lithified material held together by high-viscosity oil." Heat the oil and the grains move. Sand production is the dominant producer failure mode; observed well-failure rate ran 17 %/yr against a 5 %/yr design assumption.

How this handbook is organised

The three technical sections — geology, reservoir engineering, production engineering — each run at the same three depths, so you can read across at whatever level you need:

  • Overview — what you'd tell someone in the truck on the way to the lease.
  • Detailed breakdown — the numbers, mechanisms and methods actually used in the asset's studies.
  • Field practice — what the reviews concluded people should do, including the mistakes already paid for.

Make it yours. Every section takes notes — hit + Note in any section heading and what you write is saved in this browser and reappears next time you open the page. The Notes button at the bottom right collects them all in one place, with export and import so you can move them between machines or back them up. The checklists in §11 tick and stay ticked. Nothing you write leaves your browser.

Read the vintages before you quote a number

These documents span 2004 to 2023 and each snapshot is true only for its date. The Phase I East Flank project appears twice — 28 % recovery at 0.57 PVI in the 2010 review, 38 % at 1.47 PVI in the 2018 review — and both are correct. Recovery, OSR, pattern counts and rates all move. Treat every figure here as the value at the date of that study, and pull today's from the live databases before it goes in a deck. The asset has also changed hands: the studies are Aera-era, and Lost Hills now sits inside CRC's portfolio.

What the completions look like

All three reservoirs are developed the same way: vertical wells, gravel-packed slotted liner. Injectors use limited-entry perforations — which is why you cannot cycle steam into an injector, and why injector completion design is effectively a one-shot decision until the next redrill.

02

Geology

The Lost Hills Anticline is a faulted, asymmetrical, doubly plunging fold trending NW–SE. Three stacked reservoirs sit on it, each trapped by a different mechanism, all charged from the same source.

Overview

One structure, three traps, one oil

The San Joaquin Basin is a former fore-arc basin bounded by the Sierra Nevada to the east and the Coast Ranges to the west. Oil at Lost Hills was generated in Kreyenhagen and Temblor source rocks in the Buttonwillow depocenter to the east, and migrated up fractures into the structure. Pyrochromatography on Tulare, Upper Etchegoin and Lower Etchegoin oil samples returns nearly identical fingerprints — strong evidence that Tulare oil leaked up through the basal unconformity from the Etchegoin below it rather than arriving separately.

Above that unconformity the Pleistocene Tulare was deposited as a fluvio-lacustrine sequence sourced from the Temblor Range 20 miles west. Below it the Pliocene Etchegoin was deposited in estuarine-to-shallow-marine conditions — diatomaceous siltstones and claystones with fine silty sand interbeds. The three traps are genuinely different, and that difference drives everything downstream:

  • Tulare — anticlinal closure with interbedded siltstone and claystone as internal seals. Productive on the northeast flank, bounded by air sands on the axis to the southwest and the aquifer to the northeast.
  • Upper Etchegoin — trapped at the angular unconformity at the base of the Tulare.
  • Lower Etchegoin — structural trap; low permeability, and the one interval where fracturing has been raised as a possible completion improvement.
Detailed breakdown

Structure

The anticline is the southern end of a 60-mile fold extending through the Kettleman Hills. The southwest limb dips 30° or less; the northeast limb dips 40–50°. Inside the heavy-oil area of interest, though, the Tulare is much gentler: shallow dip near the axis, roughly 5–7° NE on the flank, with the axis plunging 1–2° SE. That 6.5° flank dip is not cosmetic — it is the number used to set injector placement (see §03).

Medwedeff described the structure as a northeast-verging growth fault-bend fold, with major slip from late Miocene to late Pleistocene. Wickham's later review of additional seismic concluded Medwedeff had picked the top of the Monterey too high, and reinterpreted the thrust as cutting through the Reef Ridge at 45–50° rather than flattening into layering, with a wedge and backthrusting at depth to reconcile the displacement. Underlying units are heavily normal-faulted; the Tulare itself is largely unfaulted.

Tulare stratigraphy and depositional model

Three sand packages, nine markers, one modelled unconformity. The depositional story explains their geometry:

  • T3 — transgressive shoreline facies laid across the eroded topographic high of the anticline. Thins over the axis (the fold was already high ground before the Tulare arrived) and is most massive immediately above the unconformity.
  • T2 — deposited during the same transgressive-to-maximum phase; like T3 it occurs in narrow lenses subparallel to the fold axis. Individual sands thin upward and get dirtier upward.
  • The maximum transgression is marked by a northeastward-thickening claystone wedge that separates T1 from T2 — your most reliable internal seal.
  • T1 — the regressive progradational package that filled the basin. It blankets T2 and T3 with relatively consistent gross and net thickness across the property, implying a nearly horizontal depositional surface. Fine to very fine grained, subangular, very friable, 1–3 D.

Sand geometry overall: lacustrine deltaic mouth bars, stacked channels and overbank deposits — sheets and troughs. Both the horizontal and vertical distribution trends "directly affect steam distribution and subsequent oil production," which is the whole reason the geologic model exists.

Air sands — the defining hazard

Air sands were identified from core and from density–neutron crossover. Near the axis they share a common base elevation of about 300–310 ft subsea, suggesting gravity drainage of oil as the structure was uplifted. The geologic model simplifies this to a single air–oil contact at 310 ft SSE across all zones. Their significance is operational, not academic: an air sand in communication with an injection interval is a thief zone and a steam-to-surface pathway.

In most of the field the air sands sit in the Tulare Rider, which isn't produced. But toward the crest — the westernmost wells — air sands extend down into the producing T1. The 1991 North Tulare review called this out explicitly for the 5137-13 and 5039-13 pattern areas and concluded: future drilling must take air-sand depth into account. Stratigraphic test well TOL5512-12 also found desaturation below the T1 oil zone, which was interpreted as a withdrawn water table with mudstones ponding oil above the air sand — and which motivated modelling a permeability barrier (a fault) in that area.

Petrophysics and the 3D model

The Tulare has little clay but does contain diatomite rip-up clasts, so grain density varies. The model handles that with a density-derived grain density and an Archie saturation, tied to core from nine wells:

Tops came from 696 wells; property logs from 589. Every zone carries its own air–oil and oil–water contacts.

Know what the model is not for

The Tulare geologic model was explicitly not designed for numerical reservoir simulation, and no geologic model existed that could be imported into the simulator. Every simulation study in these documents had to hand-build simplified layer-cake geology, and the modellers hit convergence errors when they tried to carry real heterogeneity through. If someone asks for a "full-field simulation," that gap is the first thing to raise.

Field practice

What a geologist on this asset actually does

  • Pick air sands before you pick a well path. On every proposed location, log the air-sand depth and set the casing shoe at the top of normally saturated pay. Historic practice on the LH2B project was exactly this.
  • Complete below an air sand only with a confining shale between. The 1991 North Tulare review made this a standing recommendation, and paired it with: run bond logs, because improperly cemented casing is a steam-migration path independent of the perforations.
  • Use CNL logs to map the steam chest. Observation-well CNLs plus open-hole logs from redrills are the asset's primary saturation surveillance. "Map the steam chests — CNL logs" is a standing surveillance instruction.
  • Beware propagated correlations. The UIC review found Aera's Etchegoin tops in Section 12 deviating significantly from an independent interpretation, with the angular unconformity relationship not preserved — the suspicion being that markers were propagated top-down instead of bottom-up. Correlate up from the unconformity.
  • Recovery factors over 100 % mean the OOIP is wrong, not the production. MD Tulare 77/81 booked 95.3 % RE with an ultimate of ~105 %; UE South patterns show 152 % and 178 %. Conservative porosity and saturation cutoffs, plus edge wells with 100 % allocation drawing fluid from outside the pattern, both push apparent RE up. Take it as a volumetric flag.
03

Reservoir engineering

Lost Hills does not history-match a simulator. It forecasts with empirical pattern-group analogues and type curves, and it manages with three ratios: pore volumes injected, steam flux, and oil–steam ratio.

Overview

The mental model

A pattern's life has three phases, and every decision maps to one of them:

Phase 1

Early

  • Primary ~14 BOPD per producer in the T1
  • Cyclic steam ~26 BOPD per producer at 100 BS/NFP cycles, lasting ~5 months per cycle
  • Injectivity is constrained by cold viscous oil until 0.28–0.37 PV injected
  • Peak arrives ~16 months and 0.27 PV after continuous injection starts at 1.5 flux
Phase 2

Plateau

  • Hold target steam flux; watch OSR, produced steam and flowline temperature
  • Ends when depletion forces the first steam cut — ~0.80 PVI at a 1.5 flux
  • Real projects have run longer than forecast whenever actual flux came in under target
Phase 3

Decline

  • Steam declines with oil to hold OSR above the economic limit
  • Nominal decline 15.8 % from analogue pattern groups
  • Terminate below 5 BOPD per producer or at ultimate recovery
The single most useful plot on the asset

Recovery efficiency vs. pore volumes of steam injected (ER vs. Qis), normalized to exclude primary. Over the analogue pattern groups it plots as a near-straight line, which means the slope is the instantaneous oil–steam ratio. Every project gets benchmarked on this one chart, and it is how you tell "mature" from "underperforming" at a glance: 1991 North Tulare sits at 24 % RE for 0.6 PVI where the analogues say it should be over 40 %.

Pattern configuration: why seven-spot

The seven-spot's advantages compound in exactly the conditions Lost Hills has: it starts at double the initial rate (2:1 producer-to-injector, no interference early), delivers a peak over 25 % higher, and ends 6 % higher on ultimate recovery through better sweep. Crucially it is also less exposed to the early injectivity constraint — twice as many producers means twice as many points to cycle steam into the reservoir while the rock is still cold. The trade is capital: in a low-price environment the Upper Etchegoin Section 19 study chose the cheaper 2.5-acre five-spot despite knowing seven-spots perform slightly better.

How forecasts actually get built here

  1. Select analogue pattern groups by vintage, configuration and structural position — and disqualify the ones that would mislead. The documented disqualifications: TulrSouth 1991/1993 (fed by fluid migration from the old in-situ combustion project), Tulr2B 1994 and TulrInfill 2000 (surrounded by active floods), TulrNorth 1991 (pressure-constrained, air-sand thieves, steam-to-surface), Tulr2B 1990 (too high on structure, thin sands, no dip), TulrEtch2B 1990 (commingled Tulare + UE).
  2. Pick wells by SoφH, pull ~28 years of history, date-normalize, and run Monte Carlo (Crystal Ball, later IHS Harmony) for P10/P50/P90.
  3. Build the type curve and, when transferring between areas, scale by the SoφH ratio — Section 19's Upper Etchegoin curve is the Section 13 curve times 0.56.
  4. Cross-check the heat with a Vogel analysis: convert the project's BTU requirement into a steam rate and compare it to what's actually being injected. Chronic over-injection against Vogel means vertical leakage to non-productive zones or lateral leakage out of the flood.

PVI is a sand-level number, not a project-level one

This is the most repeated lesson in the reviews. A project-level PVI of 1.61 (MD 77/81) "alone is an indication that the entire area is depleted" — until you break it out by sand and find remaining opportunity in T1B and T3B. Conversely Phase II sat at 0.54 project PVI while T1A was already at 0.74, which justified cutting steam despite a forecast that said cut at 0.80. Phase III sat at exactly the 0.45 PVI its forecast said to cut at, and did not cut, because the thickest sand (T1B) was only at 0.41. The tool for this is the SSIC — Subsurface Injection Conformance — Segment Group Volumes.

Field practice

Decision rules the reviews actually applied

  • Never cut on project PVI alone
    Break PVI out by sand at pattern level, then decide. Also weigh produced steam, flowline temperature, RE, oil saturation logs and water-disposal constraints — Phase III listed all five as reasons to hold rate.
  • Strong OSR outranks a desaturated log
    At pattern 5052-13 the open-hole log showed every sand desaturated, yet the well produced strongly (possible heat suppression). With OSR still strong and flux moderate, the team held rate and put the injector on a watch list for future cuts instead.
  • High temperature plus produced steam equals over-injection
    Ten Phase II injectors were cut on this signal, for a total 670 BSPD reduction that moved the project toward its Vogel heat requirement.
  • Cold patterns with intact saturation are redevelopment, not repair
    Six TOWs across 1991 North Tulare showed near-intact oil and cold temperatures at 0.6 PVI. That is not a well problem; it is a project that was never processed.
  • Pattern size is a hard-won constant
    Five-acre patterns at 1991 North Tulare could not be processed at the available injection pressure. 3.25 acres is the standard for Tulare development, and 2.5-acre five-spots for the Upper Etchegoin.
  • Don't perforate the T1A
    The recurring failure mode across the East Flank is a T1A thief zone. Newer redrills deliberately exclude T1A and the top of T1B; the North Tulare review recommends the top perforation sit at or below the bottom half of the T1B to raise the allowable pressure and stay away from air sands.
04

Production engineering

Shallow, unconsolidated, hot and heavy. Sand control, casing integrity and the vapour-recovery system are where most of the barrels are won and lost.

Overview

What a well looks like and how it fails

Every producer and injector across the three reservoirs is a vertical well with a gravel-packed slotted liner; injectors carry limited-entry perforations. Producers are rod-pumped and are supposed to be connected to the CVR (casing vapour recovery) system. Failure modes, in rough order of how often they appear in the pattern reviews:

  1. Sanding. Stuck pumps, short run life, liner failures, and ultimately redrills. Design assumed 5 %/yr well failures; actual was 17 %/yr. Cause: shallow formation → low compressive strength, plus the drag force from grains released as oil viscosity drops with heat.
  2. Casing damage and holes. Parted casing at 288 ft, a hole at 53 ft taking all the steam, damage at 218–335 ft blocking survey tools. Shallow damage is common and often only found by a survey that won't pass.
  3. High pickup. Fill, scale or damage that stops the survey tool above the perforations, so you can no longer measure the injection profile. Cleanouts, mud motors and slim flex tools are the escalation ladder; if none work the well goes on the redrill list.
  4. Thief-zone development. Almost always the T1A. Patch, and if the patch fails or can't be gauged, redrill and exclude the interval.
  5. Surface equipment. Cyclonic control valve failures, plugged instrument tubing, bad PLCs and dead transmitters — repeatedly the actual reason a "reservoir" problem showed up.

The CVR system is a production variable, not plumbing

The Phase I review is unambiguous on this. Wells that had their CVR closed and then reopened did not recover to their previous rate (LH4076R-13); wells choked to control produced steam took severe production hits (LH4077R-13, LH4054-13). The MD 77/81 review found the opposite lever equally real: the MD CVR system was shut down in 2001 as uneconomic to repair, a small group of wells was reconnected in 2012, the results were good enough that reconnecting became a ranked candidate list, and the flood review was used to prioritise it. Treat CVR status as a first-class item on every well you look at.

Cyclic steam and its eligibility rule

Cyclic steam is how cold wells get connected and how new patterns get started. A cycle is sized around 100 BS/NFP in the East Flank design; the cycle ends when flowline temperature drops to about 120 °F, at which point production falls off sharply. But there is a hard constraint you will hit constantly in the older projects: producers completed before ~2000 are generally not cyclic-steam candidates — the aged completions won't take steam. In 1991 North Tulare that rule alone removed cyclic steam as an option across most of the project, which is a large part of why it never responded.

Field practice

Habits that separate a good review from a list of numbers

  • Check the surface equipment before you conclude anything about the reservoir. Repeatedly, a "pattern problem" was a failed control valve. In 2016 several cyclonic control valves had to be replaced across the East Flank and injection targets were constrained for most of the year; rate measurement was simultaneously corrupted by faulty PLCs. One survey read 27 BSPD where the records said 118 BSPD — the records were wrong.
  • Prove the redrill before you drill the neighbours. The reviews routinely make producer redrills contingent on the results of an injector redrill or an offset well. Write the contingency down.
  • Use offset open-hole logs as free saturation data. Where no TOW exists in a pattern, recent redrill open-hole logs and even Williamson wells drilled through the interval carry the CNL information you need.
  • Escalate before compliance forces your hand. LC5176-13 had no compliant survey from 2008 to 2017; it took a special flex tool to get a profile below the top perforation, which reset the clock for five years and moved the well up the redrill priority. Being out of survey compliance is what idles injectors permanently.
  • Record the reasoning, not just the decision. A cancelled patch at pattern 5153-13 was documented with the alternative (an ~$18,000 wellhead replacement) and the trigger to revisit it, explicitly "so we don't lose track of our thought process." That habit is why these reviews are still usable a decade later.
05

Steam and surveillance

Steam is the scarce resource, the safety hazard and the main surveillance signal all at once. Everything about how it is generated, delivered and measured matters to the reservoir.

Overview

The system and its constraints

  • Capacity. Nominal 30,000 BSPD; realistic ~27,500 BSPD, and the binding constraint is water treatment capacity feeding the generators, not the generators. As of the 2016 study the field ran at ~80 % of plant maximum. Drill schedules in the development pipeline are explicitly annotated "depends on steam availability."
  • Distribution. A 10″ main feeds all of Lost Hills Heavy, branching to 6″ and 4″ lines. The 2010 rework insulated all three sizes, repaired damaged pipe, and replaced top-of-branch tapping with a hard T-split for uniform distribution. Quality went from ~52 % to 77 % on Phases I–II and 89 % on Phase III. Distance to the generators is a real variable: 1991 North Tulare's poor performance is partly attributed to it.
  • Drive pressure. Historically above 500 psi; from 2017 it slid to a sustained low around 300 psi. Aim for ~900 psi manifold pressure as the surveillance standard.
  • Quality improvement buys plateau, not just heat. Both the Phase I and Phase II reviews attribute their extended production plateau directly to the quality improvement, and both used a Vogel heat balance to argue the same BTUs could then be delivered with less steam: Phase I 3,709 → 3,115 BSPD, a 593 BSPD cut.

06

The pattern-review loop

The Recovery Process Evaluation is the asset's core recurring deliverable and the reason these documents exist. Learn its shape and you understand most of what the team does.

An RPE (also called a flood review) is run by a multidisciplinary team — a reservoir engineer, a geologist and a production engineer — at project level, then pattern level, then well level. Its purpose is to assess RE, PVI and OSR; review subsurface injection conformance and prescribe corrective action; re-set steam injection targets; and identify redrill, lift-optimisation and stimulation candidates.

Toolset

OpenWorks · EarthVision · Petrel · the subsurface injection conformance (SSIC) tool · DSS · OpenWells · WRA · LEGO · ArcGIS · DIMS · Business Objects · IHS Harmony · PEEP (case building and scheduling) · Tableau (base decline) · Excel. On the regulatory side, WellSTAR for CalGEM submissions.

The planning cycle around it

The RPE feeds the annual rhythm: pattern review → flood review → reservoir model → depletion plan → AFE → operating plan → reserves. Practical notes from the asset's own transition material:

  • Base decline is carried in Tableau; projects are added to the PVUD C2 case.
  • PEEP cases are built per injector and producer, then run through Scheduler and consolidated. An AFE typically carries four cases.
  • Rules of thumb used for quick forecasts: field decline ~8 %, water decline ~3 %, water cut ~91 %, roughly 200 BWPD per Tulare well and 400 BWPD per Etchegoin well, steam from OSR, and 1P/3P bracketing the mid case at roughly 80 % / 120 %.
  • AFE cost estimates split tangible vs non-tangible and drilling vs facilities. Request an XPT test on a development package to inform completion design.
  • Royalty matters to project selection: the LH3, LHC and MD fee properties carry no royalty; the West Government Lease carries a royalty (12.5 % in the later reviews; a 6.5 % overriding royalty with participation in a reduction program in the 2004 model — confirm the current terms with Land before you use either). Any well drilled within 75 ft either side of the Section 13/18 line splits production half to each.

How to read the pairs

High PVI + high RE (MD 77/81, UE South 90/91): mature, near economic limit, current OSR looks flatteringly high only because steam has been cut. Value here is in reconnecting CVR, sand-level infill and replacing packer injectors — not in blanket steam.

Low PVI + low RE (1991 North Tulare): the project was never processed. This is redevelopment territory — smaller patterns, better completions, and the explicit lesson to avoid the T1A.

Mid PVI + on-trend RE (EF Phases I–III): the working part of the flood. Manage flux, conformance and produced steam; expect the plateau to run longer than forecast whenever actual flux lands under target.

Regulatory and UIC

The injection pressure gradient is the single regulatory number that shapes field development here, and its history at Lost Hills is written in blowouts.

Overview

Project lineage

Lost Hills Heavy today sits under UIC project 43200022 (Tulare/Etchegoin steamflood, Sections 13, 18, 19), which is the merger of several older projects. The separate light-oil waterflood at Lost Hills 1 is 43200025. Knowing the lineage matters because approvals, pressure limits and zone definitions were granted against the predecessor project numbers.

  • 1962–1990In-situ (wet) combustion project operating in the LH2 / Section 19 area. Fluid influx from it still explains anomalous performance in the South 1991 and 1993 pattern groups.
  • 197543200004 — Getty, Tulare steamflood, Section 13.
  • 197743200012 and 43200034 — Getty, Etchegoin, Section 13 (dual steamflood/waterflood and steam injection).
  • 1983–1985Gulf Oil — Tulare steamflood, then cyclic steam in Section 18.
  • 1989Mobil requests a separate Etchegoin gradient (Tulare 0.6, Etchegoin 0.8 psi/ft) with zones isolated by separate injectors — DENIED.
  • Aug 1990Tubing-and-packer variance granted; higher injection pressure allowed for limited-entry injectors: MASIP 1.1 psi/ft at 100 % steam quality, with liquid fluid levels no higher than the bottom perforation.
  • Jan 1991Alternative added: 0.6 psi/ft at the sand interface, with steam quality not to drop below 30 %.
  • Apr 19911.1 psi/ft rescinded after multiple blowouts. Injection capped at 0.6 psi/ft.
  • May 19911.1 psi/ft reinstated — the blowouts were attributed to operational error and abandonment issues rather than the gradient itself.
  • 1991–2007Successive expansions across Sections 13, 18 and 19 under Mobil and then Aera; 43200022 formed by merging 43200004 and 43200034 in Aug 2002.
  • 2018DOGGR recognises a zone-name change on 43200025 from Tulare/Etchegoin to Etchegoin/Reef Ridge — the most recent (2005) pressure approval still carries the old name.
  • 2021Steam-to-surface event in the Lower Etchegoin.
Field practice

What this means for your day job

  • The gradient is why pattern sizes are small. Large patterns require high injection rates; shallow targets cap the pressure; the two are irreconcilable. That is the whole 1991 North Tulare story, and one operator chose to break the pressure limit deliberately (LH5035) and put steam on the ground.
  • Packer injectors are a compliance dead end. Injectors need periodic steam injection surveys; a tubing-and-packer completion prevents running one. Across MD 77/81 and UE South 90/91, this single fact idled almost every original injector. The survivors are slim-hole replacements completed without packers, and new injection in those areas requires drilling new injectors. (The reviews cite a two-year cadence in one place and five years in another — confirm the current CalGEM requirement rather than either.)
  • Poorly abandoned wells are a live crossflow and steam-to-surface risk. The MD 77/81 review attributes unexplained production increases to possible crossflow between the Etchegoin and Tulare through poorly abandoned wells near water-disposal wells. The UE Sec 19 study makes an AOR review of a 250 ft radius a pre-condition of drilling, with any suspect wells re-abandoned first.
  • Marker and zone definitions are contested. The UIC work flagged that "EST markers are crossing existing markers used for UIC project review," that Williamson has been redefined into the Reef Ridge, and that the Tulare strat column in the viewer appears to have T1/T2/T3 out of order. Never assume the marker set in a database matches the one a UIC project was approved against.
  • Scale of the current review. The 43200022 project-by-project review covers 150 development wells and 2,198 wellbore diagrams. Budget accordingly.

The Green Zone constraint

Maximum surface disturbance has been reached in Section 18. The plan from around 2018 is to bank surface east of the Green Zone to allow future disturbance, which requires the swapped area to be fully restored. Drought and lack of investment in watering the area have kept it from reaching that standard — so this is a real, live blocker on the East Flank Phase 4 package, not a formality.

Where the growth is

  • North and west — limited until more leases are acquired; a north lease purchase was rejected by Crimson.
  • East — limited pending a land swap with BLM.
  • South — currently viable.
  • Commingling — the overview recommends future commingling of Upper Etchegoin and Tulare, and several existing wells near the crest already produce both.
  • Idle-well sidetracks — 169 idle wells reviewed at 100 %, 29 candidates submitted (17 %), 4 online at ~100 BOPD, and ~30 more with future potential. Cheap barrels relative to new drills.
  • Recent drilling — 57 producers drilled since 2018 delivering ~1,000 BOPD and 6,800 BWPD at 87 % water cut.

Field-proven lessons

Twelve conclusions this asset has already paid for. Most of them recur across independent reviews a decade apart, which is what makes them worth memorising.

  • Don't oversize patterns
    Five-acre patterns at 1991 North Tulare were drilled large to save money and then could not be processed within the pressure limits. 3.25 acres is the Tulare standard.
  • Avoid the T1A
    Every recurring conformance failure on the East Flank is a T1A thief. Newer redrills exclude T1A and the top of T1B; put the top perforation in the lower half of the T1B.
  • Never inject where you can't survey
    Packer injectors, high pickup and casing damage all end the same way: no compliant profile, then a shut-in injector. Design and maintain for surveyability.
  • A patch buys time, not a solution
    Patches at 5028-13, 5032-13, 5055-13, 5129-13 and 5153-13 either failed, leaked, or couldn't be gauged through the wellhead. Plan for the redrill.
  • Steam quality is a reservoir variable
    52 % → 77–89 % extended the production plateau on two independent projects and allowed a Vogel-justified reduction in steam volume. Measure it; a 19 % reading is a broken valve upstream, not a reservoir fact.
  • Complete below air sands, behind shale
    And run bond logs. Steam migrating outside the pipe to a shallow air sand is a documented cause of steam-to-surface, independent of where you perforated.
  • Cyclic steam has an expiry date
    Producers completed before ~2000 generally will not take steam. If a cold pattern is full of pre-2000 completions, cyclic isn't a lever — redevelopment is.
  • Watch the CVR like a choke
    Closing or choking a CVR to manage produced steam costs real oil, and wells often do not fully recover when it reopens. Reconnecting orphaned producers has repeatedly paid.
  • Benchmark on RE vs PVI
    One plot ranks every project in the field. If a project is below the trend at its PVI, the cause is nearly always absent steam, oversized patterns, or conformance — in that order.
  • Forecast at sand level
    A project-level PVI target for cutting steam will be wrong in both directions. Phase II cut at 0.54; Phase III held at 0.45. Both were right, because their sand-level distributions differed.
  • Assume the failure rate is 17 %, not 5 %
    Sanding in shallow, unconsolidated, heated rock. Build it into well-count, workover and redrill planning up front.
  • Write down the depletion strategy
    Multiple reviews open with "this team was unable to locate such a document." Forty years later, that absence still costs the asset money. Document injection strategy, pattern configuration, completion details and forecasts — for whoever inherits this next.

Your first 90 days

A sequence that front-loads the things you can only learn from people and data access, and defers the reading you can do any time.

Days 1–15

Orientation and access

  • Get accounts on OpenWorks, Petrel/EarthVision, DSS, OpenWells, WRA, PEEP, IHS Harmony, ArcGIS, WellSTAR and the SSIC tool. Access latency is the single biggest ramp-up risk.
  • Read this handbook end to end, then the 2023 Lost Hills Heavy Overview deck for the current picture.
  • Walk the field. See a producer, an injector wellhead, a TOW, the CVR line, the steam manifold and the water plant.
  • Build your own well list by reservoir and status, and learn the naming conventions: lease prefix, number, section suffix, R/R1 for redrills.
  • Meet the operations, steam, water, EHS/regulatory and drilling counterparts. Ask each what they wish reservoir engineering understood better.
Days 16–45

Get fluent in one project

  • Pick one East Flank phase and reproduce its RPE from raw data: volumetrics, production, injection, PVI by sand, RE vs PVI.
  • Read the two extremes back to back — MD Tulare 77/81 (mature, high RE) and 1991 North Tulare (cold, unprocessed). Everything else falls between them.
  • Sit in on a pattern review and the LHH steam injection monthly meeting. Take the actions list and follow three items to completion.
  • Learn the SSIC tool properly — it is the backbone of every conformance argument you'll make.
  • Pull the type log for each reservoir and be able to pick T1A/1B/1C, T2, T3, B2A/B/C and the base-Tulare unconformity yourself.
Days 46–90

Own something

  • Take a pattern group of your own: set injection targets, defend them with SSIC + PVI by sand + Vogel, and track the response.
  • Work one development package through the chain — reservoir model → depletion plan → AFE → PEEP cases → reserves.
  • Build the steam-availability picture: what capacity exists, what the water plant limits, and what that means for the drill schedule.
  • Get involved in the 43200022 UIC project review and the AOR workflow. Regulatory literacy is a differentiator here.
  • Produce one written artifact — a pattern review, an RPE section, or a depletion-strategy note for a project that lacks one.
Questions worth asking in week one

What is the current steam allocation and who arbitrates it? · What is today's manifold and drive pressure, and has the post-2017 slide been recovered? · How many injectors are currently out of survey compliance? · What is the live status of the Green Zone / BLM swap? · Which patterns are on the current steam-cut and steam-restore lists? · Where does the 43200022 project-by-project review stand with CalGEM? · Which projects still have no documented depletion strategy?

Glossary

Terms and abbreviations used constantly in Lost Hills documents, in the sense this asset uses them.

AOR
Area of Review — the radius around an injection project in which wells must be evaluated for integrity and proper abandonment.
BSPD / BS/NFP
Barrels of steam per day; barrels of steam per net foot of pay (the way cyclic steam volumes are sized).
CER
Capital expenditure request — the naming prefix on development packages (T22-LH-A and so on).
CNL
Compensated neutron log — the saturation log used to map desaturation and steam chests.
CVR
Casing vapour recovery — collects produced steam from producer casing annuli; also the direct measurement of steam breakthrough.
DCA
Decline curve analysis, run in DSS at Lost Hills.
DOGGR / CalGEM
The California oil and gas regulator; renamed from DOGGR to CalGEM in 2020. Older documents say DOGGR throughout.
Flux
Steam injection rate normalised to bulk reservoir volume, in BSPD per acre-foot. The primary injection control variable.
FOP
Fluid over pump, in feet. Non-zero FOP means the well is not pumped off and there may be lift upside.
Green Zone
Surface-disturbance-constrained area in Section 18; drilling there depends on banking restored acreage elsewhere.
High pickup
The depth at which a survey tool stops, above the intended perforations — fill, scale or casing damage. Blocks profile measurement.
ISI
Idle-status indicator on a wellbore; paired with TA/PA in the review tables.
Limited entry
Injector completion using restricted perforations to distribute steam across intervals. Precludes cycling steam into that well.
MASIP
Maximum allowable surface injection pressure — expressed at Lost Hills as a gradient in psi/ft.
NFP
Net feet of pay.
OSR / BOSR
Oil–steam ratio; breakeven OSR. A 2018 East Flank breakeven was quoted around 0.03, with an economic screening threshold of ~0.11 used when curtailing.
PVI / Qis
Pore volumes of steam injected. The clock of a steamflood.
RE / ER
Recovery efficiency — cumulative oil over STOOIP.
Rider
The sand package above T1A. Carries volume, is not fluxed, and frequently contains air sand.
RPE
Recovery Process Evaluation — the multidisciplinary flood review that is this asset's core deliverable.
Soφh / SoφH
Oil saturation × porosity × net thickness — the hydrocarbon-pore-thickness map used to select analogues and rank locations.
SSIC
Subsurface injection conformance tool — gives pore volumes injected by segment group (i.e. by sand).
STOOIP
Stock tank original oil in place, from the 3D geologic model.
Steam chest
The heated, desaturated volume around an injector. Mapped with CNLs and temperature surveys.
Thief zone
An interval taking steam disproportionately, starving the pay. At Lost Hills this usually means the T1A or an air sand.
TOW
Temperature observation well — unproduced wells logged for temperature and saturation over time.
UNEC
Uneconomic — a well status, not necessarily permanent.
Vogel analysis
The heat-balance method used here to convert a project's BTU requirement into a defensible steam injection rate.
Williamson
A deeper sand interval; wells drilled through it supplied CNL saturation data for the Upper Etchegoin reviews. Recently redefined into the Reef Ridge for UIC purposes.
